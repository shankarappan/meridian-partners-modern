Changes to the Overseas Investment Act: What Do They Mean?

Adelina Ong explains recent Overseas Investment Act changes and the considerations for overseas purchasers of qualifying New Zealand residential property.

By Adelina Ong13 May 20265 min read

In this section

All articlesProperty & overseas investmentMeridian article

The Overseas Investment Act 2005 is important not only for overseas investors, but also for New Zealand’s legal and property sectors and economy. Key changes to the Act came into force on 6 March 2026, including changes to the investment-visa pathways for overseas investors. Certain investor-visa holders are permitted to buy qualifying residential land where the purchase price exceeds $5 million.

To better understand these changes, this article addresses several important questions.

Who can buy in New Zealand?

The changes state that holders of the Investor 1 and Investor 2 visas (in effect from July 2009 to 2022) and holders of an Active Investor Plus visa (from July 2022) have a right to purchase and invest in New Zealand. They do not have to invest personally and may instead invest through companies, trusts and other entities. For these entities, the visa holder must control at least 25% of the entity, and an overseas person who does not hold one of the specified investor visas cannot control more than 25%. For trust property, only investor-visa holders and their families may have a beneficial interest.

What kind of residential land can you buy?

Residential or lifestyle, but not sensitive

The land must be categorised as “residential” or “lifestyle” for the purposes of the District Valuation Roll, a dataset prepared by local authorities. This classification is not based on the zoning of the land. A property in an area zoned as residential is not necessarily a residential property for this purpose, so investors must refer to the District Valuation Roll. Free online services, including QV, can provide information normally found in a District Valuation Roll.

The land must not be “sensitive land”. Sensitive land includes non-urban land greater than five hectares; land greater than 0.2 hectares adjoining the seabed or foreshore; land that includes the seabed or foreshore; and land greater than 0.4 hectares on certain islands. As before the changes, a Sensitive Land Certificate can confirm whether land is sensitive. If the land has a share in common over sensitive land, such as a shared driveway, the land is also considered sensitive.

Dwellings

Land purchased must either have a habitable dwelling on it or have a dwelling built on it within three years of purchase, unless there is a valid reason for an extension. A shed or dilapidated house is not sufficient.

What does the $5 million minimum mean?

The property’s purchase price must be over $5 million. This is either the price of the land and an existing dwelling, or the price of bare land plus the cost of building a dwelling on it.

If an investor obtains Overseas Investment Office approval to build based on plans estimated to cost more than $5 million, and then reduces the build enough to place the transaction below $5 million, the OIO may reverse its consent and require the investor to cease ownership of the property.

The Purchase Price Allocation rules apply to the sale and purchase of these properties. Vendors and purchasers must agree on the portion of the purchase price attributable solely to land and buildings. Legal and tax advice will be required when determining the purchase-price allocation.

Purpose of purchase

Unless there are previous rulings, purchase under the investor-visa pathway does not restrict the use or intended use of the property. The purchaser does not need to reside there permanently; the property may, for example, be used as a holiday home or subdivided and developed.

What about the national interest?

Purchases must undergo a National Interest Assessment to ensure they are not contrary to New Zealand’s national interest. A proposed purchase next to a national defence site, for example, could be regarded as potentially endangering the national interest.

If I fulfil all these criteria, can I purchase the property?

Meeting the criteria does not automatically qualify an investor to proceed. An OIO consent application must still be submitted, and consent must be obtained before going unconditional on a property purchase.

Are there other matters to consider?

There are further amendments concerning qualification to purchase residential land as a New Zealand resident. The requirements are:

  1. Hold a residence-class visa.
  2. Have resided in New Zealand for at least the preceding 12 months.
  3. Be a tax resident by having been present in New Zealand for at least 184 days in total during the immediately preceding 12 months.

Other technical provisions include rules concerning spouses and individuals purchasing through entities.
